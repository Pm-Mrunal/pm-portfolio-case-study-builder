# PM Evaluation Frameworks

> What recruiters and hiring managers are actually looking for when they read a PM portfolio.
> Used by sub-agents to calibrate review criteria.

---

## What a Recruiter Scans For in 6 Seconds

1. **Title match** — Does the role type and seniority level match the open role?
2. **Company recognition** — Is the company name recognizable or does it need context?
3. **Stage clarity** — Did this ship? Is this a real product or a student project?
4. **Scope signal** — What's the rough size and complexity of the work?
5. **Outcome headline** — Is there a number or a signal that stands out?

If any of these are unclear or missing, the recruiter moves on. They don't dig.

**The 6-second test:** Can a reader who skims the first paragraph of the TLDR answer these three questions? (1) What did this PM do? (2) Was it real, shipped work? (3) Did it work?

## What a Hiring Manager Evaluates in a Full Read

### 1. Problem Diagnosis Quality
Can this PM identify the real problem — not the symptom, not the surface request? Do they understand the business stakes of the problem? Can they distinguish between "users are complaining" and "this is causing churn at the mid-market tier"?

### 2. Role Ownership Clarity
What did this PM personally own vs. contribute to? The best PMs are honest about this. Vague "we" language reads as a red flag — it usually means either the PM can't separate their contribution from the team's, or they're inflating their role.

### 3. Decision Quality
Are there real decisions in this case? A PM who only describes what happened — not what choices were made — doesn't demonstrate judgment. The best case studies show: here were the options, here's what I ruled out and why, here's the tradeoff I accepted, here's what I chose.

### 4. Evidence and Insight
Did the PM have real user insight that drove their decisions? Not "we did research" — but "research revealed X, which changed our assumption about Y, which led us to Z." The causal chain from insight to decision is the PM craft signal.

### 5. Outcome Credibility
Are the outcomes stated with appropriate precision? Overstated outcomes ("10x improvement," "transformed the business") without context are a red flag. Honest directional outcomes with clear attribution are more credible than inflated numbers.

### 6. Reflection Quality
Does the PM demonstrate self-awareness? Can they name something specific they'd do differently? A case study that ends with "this was a huge success and I'm proud of the outcome" without any reflection reads as junior or defensive.

## PM Archetypes Hiring Managers Screen For

**The Builder (0-to-1)**
Evidence signals: Greenfield scope, ambiguity navigation, problem definition from scratch, customer discovery at early stage, decisions made without historical data, willingness to kill ideas that don't validate.

**The Grower (Growth PM)**
Evidence signals: Funnel ownership, experimentation framework, quantitative outcomes, user acquisition or retention metrics, A/B test design and results, pricing or packaging decisions.

**The Platform PM**
Evidence signals: Internal customers (engineering or other teams), API or infra thinking, developer experience, adoption within the org, tradeoffs between flexibility and standardization, build vs. buy vs. borrow decisions.

**The Enterprise PM**
Evidence signals: Customer segmentation, sales cycle integration, compliance or security considerations, multi-stakeholder decision-making, contract-gated launches, large account management.

**The Redesign / Revamp PM**
Evidence signals: Prior state articulation, user research on existing experience, what was preserved vs. changed and why, migration or adoption strategy, regression prevention.

## Common Failure Modes in PM Case Studies

**Failure mode 1: The Activity List**
Describes everything that happened without showing any decisions. Reads like a project log, not a portfolio piece. Fix: add explicit options-considered and rationale language.

**Failure mode 2: The Team Accomplishment**
Uses "we" throughout with no first-person ownership. The reader can't tell what the PM did specifically. Fix: identify at least 3 areas of explicit personal ownership.

**Failure mode 3: The Inflated Metric**
Leads with a big number without context. "$5M revenue impact" from a minor feature feels inflated. Fix: add context — team size, timeline, what baseline the number is measured against.

**Failure mode 4: The Vague Problem**
States the problem as "improve the user experience" or "drive growth." No prior state. No affected user. No business stakes. Fix: name who had the problem, what state things were in before, and why fixing it mattered.

**Failure mode 5: The Generic Reflection**
"I learned the importance of stakeholder alignment." Every PM has learned this. Fix: name what specifically was learned on this project and what would actually be done differently.

**Failure mode 6: The No-Outcome Case Study**
The case study ends at launch. Nothing about what happened. Fix: even if metrics aren't available, add validation signals, qualitative observations, or what the work enabled.

## The Causal Chain Test

A strong case study passes this test at every step:

- Problem → Users: Does the user definition explain why this problem exists?
- Users → Discovery: Did discovery actually surface something about these users?
- Discovery → Decision: Did an insight drive a specific decision?
- Decision → Solution: Does the solution reflect the decision, not just the problem?
- Solution → Outcomes: Are the outcomes connected to the solution, not just co-occurring?
- Outcomes → Reflection: Does the reflection respond to what actually happened?

If any link in this chain is missing, the case study has a causality gap — and a hiring manager will notice.
